# Research on KV State Preservation Across Compaction Boundaries

## Bottom line

The closest answer to your core hypothesis is **partial existence, not full prior art**. I found several lines of work that get near one or more pieces of the target: exact or modular KV reuse across contexts, compaction into synthetic KV states, cache editability and splicing, and even a recent asymmetric reuse result where **surviving cached chunks can be relocated by exact RoPE re-rotation while keeping their conditioned cache near-lossless**. But I did **not** find a mature, general-purpose method for **text** compaction that explicitly says: *retain the original write-time value states of retained text across a compaction boundary, refresh or relocate keys for the new context, and validate that the semantic interpretation of the retained text itself stays stable*. The most relevant evidence comes from two recent papers: **Models Take Notes at Prefill** and **Kamera**. The former shows that downstream cached “notes” preserve prior conclusions and can be transplanted or amended; the latter shows a position/conditioning separation where the reusable part can survive window motion with only exact re-rotation, plus a small patch when cross-chunk conditioning must be restored. Together, they strongly support the intuition that **write-time cached state carries semantic commitments**, but they still fall short of the full generalized scheme you described. citeturn9view0turn26view4turn26view5turn35view1turn36view0turn36view3

## What the adjacent literatures actually do

The most established family is **KV-cache eviction and compression**. StreamingLLM keeps attention sinks so streaming generation remains stable with finite cache; H2O retains recent and “heavy-hitter” tokens; SnapKV selects clustered important KV positions per head from an observation window; PyramidKV dynamically allocates different cache budgets across layers. Newer variants such as ChunkKV, SemantiCache, SentenceKV, SeKV, IntentKV, and Transactional Attention incorporate chunking, semantic grouping, hierarchical reconstruction, intent-awareness, or “sponsorship” for otherwise low-attention but structurally important tokens. These methods are mainly about **which KV entries to keep, merge, offload, sponsor, or reconstruct** under memory pressure. Even when they use words like *semantic chunk*, *semantic integrity*, or *semantic coherence*, they still target efficiency and downstream benchmark quality, not explicit preservation of the interpretation of surviving text across a compaction boundary. citeturn38search1turn38search3turn38search2turn38search0turn18search0turn18search1turn18search5turn31search0turn31search5turn12academia18

A neighboring family is **cross-context KV reuse and blending**. Prompt Cache makes reuse modular by caching reusable prompt modules and concatenating them with discontinuous position IDs; it reports comparable LongBench accuracy while primarily optimizing latency. CacheBlend reuses independently precomputed chunks and selectively recomputes a small subset of KV values so that combined caches approach full-prefill quality in RAG. EPIC formalizes position-independent caching and uses LegoLink to cut recomputation around chunk starts while retaining accuracy. KVLink concatenates independently cached documents, adjusts positional embeddings at inference, and adds trainable special tokens to recover cross-document self-attention. More recently, KV Packet wraps cached chunks with trainable soft-token adapters so they can be concatenated without inference-time recomputation. These systems are best read as **cache composition and cache repair** methods. They preserve or repair utility of reused contexts, but they are not framed as preserving the semantic interpretation of retained text across compaction specifically. citeturn15view2turn15view4turn16view0turn16view3turn14view2turn27view2turn27view4turn17view0turn17view1turn35view0

A different branch performs **learned context compression into latent or soft tokens** instead of retaining original KV states. Gist Tokens trains models to compress prompts into cacheable “gist” tokens; AutoCompressors train long contexts into compact summary vectors used as soft prompts; ICAE compresses contexts into compact memory slots; Activation Beacon directly compresses activations, including keys and values at every layer, rather than relaying meaning through soft prompts. Cartridges trains a smaller corpus-specific KV cache offline through self-study and context distillation. Attention Matching synthesizes compact keys and values to reproduce attention outputs and attention mass. Still trains a small Perceiver once to produce compact keys and values in a single forward pass, and shows that the compact cache can support summarization tasks. These methods are close in spirit if your end goal is *compact state that preserves usefulness*, but they usually **replace** the original write-time states with newly synthesized latent states rather than preserving the original value states of retained text. citeturn34view0turn34view1turn34view2turn34view3turn23view2turn23view0turn24view0turn24view1turn24view4

The most relevant neighboring line for your hypothesis is **KV-cache editability and composability**. CaM and KeepKV try to reduce output perturbation by merging evicted entries into retained ones, but their goal is still compression quality. By contrast, **Models Take Notes at Prefill** makes a much stronger mechanistic claim: the model has already written field-conditioned conclusions onto downstream tokens during prefill, so merely refreshing the changed field’s own KV does not update the decision; the model keeps acting on the old value because the **downstream notes** are what matter. The paper then shows those notes are editable and position-portable: they can be amended with an append-only erratum or spliced into new contexts as reusable skills. This is the clearest existing evidence that cached write-time state can preserve semantic commitments across reuse boundaries. citeturn25search0turn25search3turn9view0turn26view2turn26view4turn26view5

Finally, there is **cross-model and cross-agent KV or latent transfer**. DroidSpeak studies sharing KV caches across compatible fine-tuned model variants and selectively recomputes a few layers when reuse would otherwise hurt quality. LRAgent and ForkKV decompose caches into shared backbone-induced components and lightweight adapter-specific components for multi-LoRA agents. C2C, latent KV-alignment, and Latent Cache Flow learn adapters or projectors that translate one model’s KV space into another’s. LatentMAS and Agent Primitives go further and use KV or other continuous latent states as communication channels between agents. This literature is about **communication, serving, and collaboration**, not semantic continuity of retained text across compaction. citeturn32search10turn32search7turn32search0turn32search9turn22search3turn32search2turn7search2turn7search0turn7search1turn32search11

## Where the closest matches sit

The strongest mechanistic match is **Models Take Notes at Prefill**. It explicitly studies what happens when a prompt changes inside an otherwise reusable region. The key finding is that updating only the changed field’s local KV does **not** update the model’s behavior, because the decisive information was written during prefill onto downstream aggregator or delimiter tokens. The paper reports that the field’s own KV causally drives under a tiny fraction of the decision, whereas the downstream notes drive essentially all of it. It then shows that these notes are **position-portable** and can be **RoPE-repositioned and spliced** into new contexts with behavior close to full recompute; on LoCoMo it reports statistical equivalence to full recompute for several Qwen models and only a small gap on Llama-3.1-8B, with high answer-logit cosine. This is not yet a general “fresh keys + original values” compaction scheme, but it is the clearest paper saying that **semantic interpretation lives in write-time cached state** and can survive transplantation. citeturn26view5turn26view4turn26view1turn26view2

The closest asymmetric result I found is **Kamera**. Its central claim is that naive non-prefix reuse loses only the **cross-chunk conditioning** a cached chunk absorbed from its neighbors. The paper says the **direct readout** of a cached chunk is already recovered exactly “for free” by standard state-merge, while the missing part is a diffuse, low-rank residue concentrated in deep layers that matters for cross-chunk binding and multi-hop reasoning. Crucially, for **sliding-window survivors**, the paper says the surviving chunks can be relocated by **exact RoPE re-rotation only**, with near-lossless behavior; only **recalled** chunks need a fresh conditioning patch. That is surprisingly close to the asymmetric idea you highlighted: the reusable chunk’s conditioned state is substantially preserved, while the position-sensitive part is adjusted rather than re-encoded. The limitation is scope: Kamera is about **multimodal position-invariant reuse and window churn**, and the authors explicitly note that in their two-chunk dense-text setup they do not observe the same gap they see in redundant vision/video streams. citeturn36view0turn36view1turn36view2turn36view3

A softer precursor is **Prompt Cache**. It makes attention-state reuse modular, uses discontinuous positions, and argues that as long as relative token positions are preserved, cached prompt modules can be concatenated to formulate new meanings. That is a strong form of exact reuse, but it is still about **reusing precomputed modules** rather than preserving semantic continuity of retained text through compaction. Similarly, **EPIC**, **CacheBlend**, **KVLink**, and **KV Packet** all attack the “context changed but I want to reuse cache” problem, yet they do so by selective recomputation, trainable special tokens, or trainable adapters rather than by explicitly preserving original write-time value states as the object of interest. citeturn15view2turn15view5turn14view2turn16view0turn17view0turn35view0

## What the literature measures and what it mostly does not

Most long-context and KV-centric evaluations are still dominated by **efficiency metrics** and **downstream accuracy proxies**: time-to-first-token, throughput, memory footprint, LongBench, Needle-in-a-Haystack, RULER, QA accuracy, summarization quality, and benchmark suites such as SCBench. SCBench is useful here because it explicitly organizes evaluation around the KV lifecycle and covers string retrieval, semantic retrieval, global information, and multi-task settings, but it still does not isolate whether the meaning of the **surviving retained text itself** changed after compaction. citeturn28search0turn28search6turn15view3turn24view0turn24view4

A more interpretability-oriented subgroup tries to minimize **attention-output perturbation** rather than just attention scores. CaM, CriticalKV, CAOTE, OBCache, and KeepKV all frame eviction or merging partly in terms of output discrepancy or output-aware saliency. That is conceptually closer to your concern than raw attention heuristics, because it acknowledges that values and downstream outputs matter. But even here, the target is still **attention/output fidelity**, not whether a retained referent, sense, or constraint is interpreted the same way after compaction. citeturn25search0turn12search6turn12search16turn12search0turn25search3

There are two notable exceptions that edge toward your notion of semantic continuity. **Models Take Notes at Prefill** includes cross-referential stress tests and shows that if cross-referential facts are split across independently precompiled blocks, agreement with full recompute drops sharply; the interpretation only survives if the referential chain was memoized within a single block. **Kamera** explicitly distinguishes single-hop readout from multi-hop cross-chunk binding and shows that blind reuse can leave single-hop recall intact while halving multi-hop accuracy, which is a direct semantic failure mode rather than a pure speed/memory tradeoff. Those are real semantic-continuity tests, but they are still specialized and not yet a standard evaluation suite for retained-text interpretation. citeturn26view0turn26view1turn36view0turn36view3

I did **not** find a benchmark tradition that directly measures the exact things you named as first-class objects of preservation — **referent resolution, word-sense disambiguation, established stance, or persistent constraints** — while varying only the compaction or cache-manipulation scheme on the same retained text. The closest literature uses multi-hop QA, long-conversation QA, retrievable credentials, or output perturbation, which are informative but indirect proxies. citeturn26view0turn26view1turn12academia18turn28search0

## Direct assessment

My direct assessment is that work matching your core description **already exists only in fragments**. Exact cache reuse across unchanged chunks exists. Position-independent or near-position-independent reuse exists. Learned latent compaction exists. Cache editing and splicing exists. There is now strong evidence that **prefill writes semantically consequential state into downstream KV representations**, and there is at least one striking result in which **surviving cached chunks can be relocated with only exact positional correction while preserving most of their conditioned state**. But the field has **not yet converged on a general-purpose text compaction method whose explicit goal is semantic-continuity preservation of retained text via preserved write-time value states across a compaction boundary**, particularly not with a dedicated evaluation protocol around referents, disambiguation, stance, or constraint persistence. citeturn26view4turn26view5turn36view0turn36view2turn23view0turn24view0

So the best one-line answer is:

**The core idea appears to be partially present, but only as a set of adjacent mechanisms and recent mechanistic discoveries, not as an established, directly named research line.** citeturn9view0turn35view1turn28search0

## Open questions and limitations

The biggest uncertainty is **generality across modalities and redundancy regimes**. Kamera is extremely relevant, but it explicitly reports that the strongest effect lives in redundant vision/video streams and that its dense-text two-chunk setup does not show the same gap, so it should not be over-generalized to ordinary text compaction without further evidence. citeturn36view3

A second gap is the evaluation story. The field still needs a benchmark that holds surviving text fixed and asks whether compaction changes **who “he” refers to, which sense of a polysemous word is active, which policy constraint is binding, or which stance the dialogue has established**. Current benchmarks largely infer this only indirectly through QA, retrieval, multi-hop reasoning, or perturbation criteria. citeturn26view0turn28search0turn12search0

A third gap is the lack of an explicit **online summary-cache retention** method. The latent-compression papers show that directly synthesized compact states are workable, but that is not the same as creating a summary in-context and preserving the exact cache state that the summary acquired while the full history was still present. citeturn34view1turn34view2turn34view3turn24view4

Finally, the strongest mechanistic paper, **Models Take Notes**, implies a design principle that still feels underexplored: if downstream values encode memoized conclusions and later decisions read those states rather than the original source tokens, then a truly semantic-preserving compactor may need to treat **positions, keys, values, and conditioning residues as different objects**, not compress them uniformly. That principle is visible in fragments today, but I did not find a mature, standardized implementation of it for general text compaction. citeturn26view5turn26view4turn36view0
